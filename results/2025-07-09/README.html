On the differentiation between populations

J. Ignacio Lucas Lledó

2025-07-17

Introduction

One way to visualize to what extent the two main populations can be considered two species is to compare the amount of differentiation between populations with the amount of diversity within a population. In 2024-10-10 we estimated \(F_{ST}\), which suggested there is about 12.5% more diversity when comparing individuals from different populations than from the same one. But this is only one measure, based on the partition of variation within and between populations. It does not convey an intuitive meaning, and it fails to address some aspects of differentiation.

To get a more intuitive picture of differentiation, I would like to see the distribution of individual genetic differences within and between populations. Kosman, Feijen, and Jokela (2024) proposes an interesting framework, based on the distinction between diversity and dispersion, as two independent aspects of variation among populations. Diversity refers to how evenly separated the populations are, it is independent of the absolute magnitude of differences among them, and it is also independent of the diversity within the populations. Diversity represents the effective number of equally distant populations, but it is equal to 2 if there are only 2 populations. Dispersion, on the other hand, is related to the actual genetic distances between populations. Combined measures of diversity and dispersion constitute the effective numbers of different populations.

Measures of genetic distance

The first thing we need is a measure of genetic dissimilarity between individuals. Originally I attempted to use estimates of coancestry, obtained with ngsrelate. The coefficient of coancestry is defined as the probability of two homologous genes drawn from two individuals being identical by descent, that is, by sharing a recent common ancestor rather than an old one. There are problems with this measure, and I move that analysis to the appendix. First, it rapidly goes to zero if the two individuals are not related. Second, it should be estimated among members of a homogeneous population, because it depends on the selection of sites assessed to be variable, and because it depends on the allele frequencies. Thus, estimates are likely to be biased when using a mixture of two populations.

The measure of genetic distance between two individuals \(A\) and \(B\) advocated by Kosman, Feijen, and Jokela (2024) is that of equation 6 in Kosman and Jokela (2019) for individuals of any ploidy \(q\) genotyped along \(n\) loci:

\[ \delta_{AB} = \frac{1}{q}\times \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n \delta_{\mathrm{min}}(A,B; j)}{n} \]

Where \(\delta_{\mathrm{min}}(A, B; j)\) is the solution to the assignment problem, namely: matching each allele in \(A\) with it’s most similar one in \(B\), and adding up the differences. For diploids, \(\delta_{\mathrm{min}}(A,B;j)\) is 0 if the two genotypes are the same (even if both are heterozygous); 1, if one genotype is homozygous and the other heterozygous; and 2 if they are homozygous for different alleles. For one diploid locus with two alleles, \(n=1\) and \(q=2\), and \(\delta_{AB}\) would be like this:

AA Aa aa
AA 0.0 0.5 1.0
Aa 0.5 0.0 0.5
aa 1.0 0.5 0.0

In a probabilistic framework, we will use the ibs program, with -model 0 option, which delivers the estimated percentages of each of the 100 possible genotype combinations: AA_AA, AC_AA, AG_AA… GT_TT, and TT_TT between two individuals. Thus, grouping loci by combination of genotypes, we have:

\[ \delta_{AB} = \frac{1}{q}\cdot \frac{\sum_{c=AA\_AA}^{TT\_TT}n_c\cdot \delta_{\mathrm{min}}(c)}{n} =\sum_{c=AA\_AA}^{TT\_TT}P(c)\cdot \frac{\delta_{\mathrm{min}}(c)}{q} \]

Where \(n_c\) is the number of loci with the same combination \(c\) of genotypes, \(P(c)\) is the frequency of the \(c\) combination between those two individuals, and the summation is over the 100 possible genotype combinations. Note that genotypes are expressed in terms of actual nucleotides. I assume that ibs estimates the expected number of every combination of genotypes without having to infer what alleles are major and minor. There is likely no need to assume certain sites as variable, nor any need to assume variable sites are bi-allelic. This is specially important, since it means that not only drift by also mutation (if any) is contributing to the distance.

delta.min <- function(x, y) {
   stopifnot(length(x) == length(y))

Genetic distances measured among RAD loci are equivalent to those measured among alignments to the reference genome only for low to moderate values, up to 0.012 or so. Larger differences depend more on what set of loci they are measured on, with RAD loci giving lower distances. Thus, when looking differences at RAD loci, some pairs of individuals from the two populations are just as different as some pairs of individuals from the same C. pseudofarionis population. There is overlap of within and between populations distances only when using the RAD loci, which is the largest data set.

Diversity within populations

The approach used in this analysis is a genetic method of analysis of diversity. It is based on comparisons among individuals, using their whole genetic profile (Kosman 2014). This is different from the genotypic methods, which are those based on frequencies of haplotypes at one locus. And it is also different from the gene methods, which are based on allele frequencies at multiple loci (Nei’s gene diversity, \(F_{ST}\)s, and so on).

The first step in the analysis of diversity is to measure diversity within populations. I use two measures: the average distance within population, based on dissimilarity \(\delta\) (\(ADW_{\delta}\)), and Kosman’s diversity, also based on a dissimilarity, \(KW_{\delta}\).

# Average Distance Within (Kosman, 2014; eq. 13): averages the whole
# matrix of distances among individuals in a population, including
# both d(i, j) and d(j, i), and distances to selves, d(i, i).
# Here, "n" is the number of individuals in the population, deduced
# from the number of pairs (length(x)).
ADW.ref <- sapply(
   c('admixed', 'farionis', 'pseudofarionis'),
   function(i) {
      x <- distances[distances$pops == paste(i, i, sep = '_'), 'delta_ref']
      n <- (1 + sqrt(1 + 8 * length(x))) / 2
      return(mean(c(x, x, rep(0, n))))
   }
)

ADW.rad <- sapply(
   c('admixed', 'farionis', 'pseudofarionis'),
   function(i) {
      x <- distances[distances$pops == paste(i, i, sep = '_'), 'delta_rad']
      n <- (1 + sqrt(1 + 8 * length(x))) / 2
      return(mean(c(x, x, rep(0, n))))
   }
)

Kosman’s diversity within is a bit more complicated. It consists on matching every individual in the population with its most different one, so as to maximize the sum of differences. The total number of pairs is equal to the number of individuals, the pairs are made between two copies of the population, as in the entris of an \(n\times n\) matrix.

library(kableExtra)
# I create the KW function to take the "distances" d.f. as input.
KW <- function(x, pop, delta = 'delta_ref') {
   x.sel <- x[x$pops %in% pop,
              c('ind1', 'ind2', delta)]
   # Eq. 14 in Kosman (2014)
   inds <- unique(c(x.sel$ind1, x.sel$ind2))
   n1 <- length(inds)
   stopifnot(nrow(x.sel) == n1 * (n1 - 1) / 2)
   # the other hemimatrix, switchin individuals
   unfold <- data.frame(
      ind1 = x.sel$ind2,
      ind2 = x.sel$ind1,
      delta = x.sel[, delta]
   )
   names(unfold)[3] <- delta
   # the diagonal elements of the complete matrix
   diagonal <- data.frame(
      ind1 = inds,
      ind2 = inds,
      delta = rep(0, length(inds))
   )
   names(diagonal)[3] <- delta
   x.sel <- rbind(
      x.sel,
      unfold,
      diagonal
   )
   # I use pivot_wider() to turn the d.f. into matrix format
   x.wide <- tidyr::pivot_wider(
      x.sel,
      id_cols = ind1,
      names_from = ind2,
      values_from = delta
   )
   class(x.wide) <- 'data.frame'
   row.names(x.wide) <- x.wide$ind1
   x.mat <- as.matrix(x.wide[, -1])
   stopifnot(nrow(x.mat) == ncol(x.mat))
   sol <- lpSolve::lp.assign(x.mat, direction = 'max')
   return(sol$objval / n1)
}

Distances between populations

Distances between populations can also be measured in different ways. Below I implement the \(DAD_{\delta}\) and the \(KB_{\delta}\). \(DAD_{\delta}\) might be negative (Kosman 2014), and must be used carefully.

# Average distance between (ADB) (Kosman, 2014; eq. 15)
# Needed to get DAD.
ADB.ref <- sapply(
   split(distances$delta_ref, distances$pops),
   mean
)
ADB.rad <- sapply(
   split(distances$delta_rad, distances$pops),
   mean
)

# Distance of average differences, equation 16 in Kosman (2014):
pairs <- c('admixed_farionis', 'admixed_pseudofarionis', 'farionis_pseudofarionis')
DAD.ref <- sapply(
   pairs,
   function(x){
      pops <- unlist(strsplit(x, '_'))
      ADB.ref[x] - (ADW.ref[pops[1]] + ADW.ref[pops[2]]) / 2
   },
   USE.NAMES = FALSE
)
DAD.rad <- sapply(
   pairs,
   function(x) {
      pops <- unlist(strsplit(x, '_'))
      ADB.rad[x] - (ADW.rad[pops[1]] + ADW.rad[pops[2]]) / 2
   },
   USE.NAMES = FALSE
)

Kosman’s distance between populations, \(KB_{\delta}\), is more complicated. We need to solve the assignment problem: for every individual in one population we have to assign one of the other, so that every individual is paired in such a way that the sum of differences is minimized. The assignment problem is usually framed between two sets of the same size: e.g, jobs and machines, where every job must be run in a different machine. Kosman (2014) does not specify how to proceed when one sample is larger than the other. But it seems to be common practice in operational research to augment the smallest set with dummy vertices connected with zero-cost edges to all vertices of the other set. The package that can solve the assignment problem in R is called lpSolve. The challenge here is to present the distances as a square matrix.

The strategy is to first extract from distances the rows that compare individuals from the two populations; then, switch individuals 1 and 2 as needed to have only one population per column. Then, use pivot_wider() from tidyr to distribute the distances in a matrix; and finally add the rows or columns necessary to make it squared.

library(kableExtra)
KB <- function(x, pops, delta = 'delta_ref') {
   x.sel <- x[x$pops == pops,
              c('ind1', 'ind2', 'pop1', 'pop2', delta)]
   popL <- unlist(strsplit(pops, '_'))
   x.sel$indpop1 <- ifelse(
      x.sel$pop1 == popL[1],
      x.sel$ind1,
      x.sel$ind2
   )
   x.sel$indpop2 <- ifelse(
      x.sel$pop1 == popL[2],
      x.sel$ind1,
      x.sel$ind2
   )
   x.wide <- tidyr::pivot_wider(
      x.sel[, c(delta, 'indpop1', 'indpop2')],
      id_cols = 'indpop1',
      names_from = 'indpop2',
      values_from = delta
   )
   class(x.wide) <- 'data.frame'
   row.names(x.wide) <- x.wide$indpop1
   x.mat <- as.matrix(x.wide[, -1])
   n1 <- nrow(x.mat)
   n2 <- ncol(x.mat)
   if (n1 < n2) {
      x.mat <- rbind(x.mat,
                     matrix(0,
                            nrow = n2 - n1,
                            ncol = n2))
   }
   if (n1 > n2) {
      x.mat <- rbind(x.mat,
                     matrix(0,
                            nrow = n1,
                            ncol = n1 - n2))
   }
   sol <- lpSolve::lp.assign(x.mat, direction = 'min')
   return(sol$objval / min(n1, n2))
}

Diversity

Here, I follow Kosman, Feijen, and Jokela (2024), where diversity is understood as how evenly distributed the populations are, or how regular distances among them are. This only makes sense for more than two populations. I have been considering the four admixed individuals as a third population mostly to test the formulas of diversity. But this section is irrelevant because we are using data from only two biologically plausible populations.

# Eq. 2 in Kosman et al. 2024.
# Note that distances are counted twice
f <- list(
   dad.ref = DAD.ref / (2 * sum(DAD.ref)),
   dad.rad = DAD.rad / (2 * sum(DAD.rad)),
   kb.ref  = KB.ref / (2 * sum(KB.ref)),
   kb.rad  = KB.rad / (2 * sum(KB.rad))
)
H <- sapply(f, function(x) exp(-sum(x * log(x))))

# Eq. 3 in Kosman et al. 2024.
D <- (1 + sqrt(1 + 4 * H)) / 2

##  dad.ref  dad.rad   kb.ref   kb.rad 
## 2.133968 2.137500 2.141292 2.141428
# Eq. 4 in Kosman et al. 2024:
S <- 3

S  <- 3

Ep
##   dad.ref   dad.rad    kb.ref    kb.rad 
## 0.5669839 0.5687498 0.5706461 0.5707140

\(D\) is the diversity of order 1 among the three populations, and it is to be interpreted as the effective number of equally distant populations. Because the admixed population is closer to C. pseudofarionis, the values are closer to 2 than to 3.

\(E'\) is the evenness of order 1, in a scale from 0 to 1. The three populations are over half as evenly distant as they could be.

These are not interesting measures, because the admixed population is not a population of interest. If we exclude it, however, we are left with a number of populations \(S = 2\), where evenness is 1 and the effective number of equally distant populations is necessarily 2.

Other measures of diversity

The effective number of different populations estimated above is a measure of \(\beta\)-diversity that is independent of total or \(\gamma\)-diversity and also independent of the amount of diversity within populations (\(\alpha\)-diversity). A more classical approach decomposes total diversity in those other two components, so that \(\beta\)-diversity becomes defined by the difference between total and average within diversities.

Kosman, Feijen, and Jokela (2024) (equation 18 and 20) suggests two measures of differentiation susceptible of being decomposed this way: either the Kosman’s assignment-based index (\(KW_{\delta}\)), or the average difference within (the total population), \(ADW_{\delta}\). But it warns that the concavity of \(ADW_{\delta}\) is not proved (it depends on the specific dissimilarity measure, \(\delta\)), and could not be valid.

N <- length(unique(c(distances$ind1, distances$ind2)))
stopifnot(dim(distances)[1] == N * (N - 1) / 2)
