## Discussion

By extracting and assembling from our ddRAD-seq data set the sequences
corresponding to popular molecular markers, we have contextualized our
samples, from only two locations, in the broad evolutionary history of their
genus. In particular, the phylogeny of the lsrDNA gene (figure 5), for which we obtained
a much longer alignment than for cox1 or ITS2, clearly groups our samples
from Langfjordvatn with previously published *C. farionis* sequences, and our samples from
Suohpatjávri with *C. pseudofarionis* sequences, with high bootstrap
support for reciprocal monophyly. The existence of two well
differentiated ancestries, and their almost perfect correspondence with the
two localities, is confirmed by the distribution of the nuclear genetic variation
revealed with the ddRAD-seq data sets (figure 6). It is therefore straightforward
to postulate that the most frequent ancestry in Langfjordvatn corresponds with
*C. farionis*, and the most frequent ancestry in Suohpatjávri is *C. pseudofarionis*.
On the bases of the ancestry proportions estimated from genome-wide nuclear
variation, we are able to assign a species even to samples for which the
lsrDNA gene could not be included in the alignment. Hereafter, we refer to the
sampled populations by the names of their assigned species, not without noting the
following exceptions to their geographic separation: one individual in Langfjordvatn,
out of 39, belongs to *C. pseudofarionis*; four individuals in Suohpatjávri (out of 52)
belong to *C. farionis*; and four individuals (one in Suohpatjávri, three in
Langfjordvatn) are admixed, all with approximately 60% of *C. pseudofarionis*
ancestry and ~40% of *C. farionis* ancestry. Admixed individuals had neither
lower amounts of data nor higher levels of heterozygosity than the rest (data not shown).

Having established the identity of the samples, we used the large ddRAD-seq
data sets to characterize these populations. Table 1 show levels of heterozygosity
and nucleotide diversity close to 0.02 in both populations, which are relatively
high. The fact that Waterson's estimates are systematically higher than Tajima's
suggest an excess of low-frequency variants. This pattern can be explained by
recent population expansions, just as those predicted by the Bayesian fit of our
data to the two-species-coalescent-with-migration model performed with bpp. The
Bayesian analysis provides evidence of continued migration between the two
populations since their divergence. Taking migration into account, the data
favors a divergence time of 0.0029 (s.d. 0.0001) mutations per site ago, which is
lower than the prior expectation of 0.0050. If mutation rate was in the order of
1.0E-09 mutations per site per year, then the estimated divergence would correspond
to 2.9 million years.

Perhaps surprisingly, however, the estimated effective number of different populations
is 1 and the FST is between 0.10 and 0.13 (depending on the data set used). This is to
say that the genetic differences between populations are not much larger than the genetic
differences among individuals of the same population. The evidence mentioned earlier
for recent population growth, and for continued migration, help explain the apparent
contradiction. Migration must have helped maintain similar allele frequencies, especially
among common ancestral variants. Migration reduces the power of drift, and makes the two
populations look alike. At the same time, the rapid population growth entails a fast
input of new mutations: new variants at low frequency, which add subtle but noticeable
differences between the populations. As a result, the two lineages are easily recognized,
even though their genetic differences are relatively small.

The unexpected similarity between our *C. farionis* and *C. pseudofarionis* samples
could motivate doubts about the validity of the most recently defined taxon. However,
we refrain from proposing any formal taxonomic changes, because our data set includes
specimens from a single host species and from only two localities. We hope the doubts
we raise will be dispelled by more comprehensive future studies.
